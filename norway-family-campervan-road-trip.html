Billy and his family left the UK in a still-new campervan and pointed it north. Over 30 days they covered 6,500 km across Scandinavia, climbed through darkness for sunrise, slept beside one of Norway's most famous roads and collected the kind of stories their children still tell years later.

At three in the morning, while most of Norway was still asleep, Billy woke the kids, climbed into the van and drove twenty minutes towards Preikestolen. The family started walking in darkness, their torches picking out the trail one step at a time.

Preikestolen · the memory that stuck

They climbed in the dark for a sunrise they still chase.

Norway had been high on the family's list for years. But this journey meant more than finally reaching a destination. They had bought the van only around eight months earlier, and this was the first chance to see what a proper, month-long adventure in it could feel like.

The Preikestolen morning became one of those memories that changes the trips that come after it. They walked most of the route by torchlight and reached the plateau in time to watch the landscape appear with the sunrise. Billy describes it as probably the most incredible sunrise the family has ever seen.

“Harley still goes in search of the perfect sunrise whenever we travel now.”
Billy · @man_adventure_van

Years later, that sunrise still travels with them. The family remembers the famous places, but this one changed the way Harley travels: he still goes looking for the next perfect sunrise wherever they go.

Atlantic Road · favourite overnight

Then there was the morning when the view was already outside the van.

For all the big-name highlights, Billy's favourite overnight spot was wonderfully simple. They parked on the Atlantic Road with an uninterrupted view of the Storseisundet Bridge and went to sleep with one of Norway's most recognisable pieces of road engineering framed by the windows.

Waking up, opening the van and seeing that view right there was exactly what the family had wanted from campervan travel. There was no hotel corridor, no transfer and no schedule between them and the landscape. Their home had simply moved somewhere extraordinary overnight.

The reality · not every road was easy

At one point, the whole van started sliding sideways.

This was April, and the further north they travelled, the more the trip demanded from the van and from the people inside it. They had snow tyres and managed to reach the places they had planned, but some roads were still, in Billy's words, pretty sketchy.

One hidden tree root. One dent. One very memorable road.

On a snowy section the van began sliding sideways. A tree root hidden beneath the snow eventually caught the side and dented the upper sill. Elsewhere, they even had the van on three wheels. Considering what they asked of it across roughly 6,500 km, Billy says it performed perfectly.

The mechanical drama never became the story of the holiday. The dent was fixable. The van kept moving. Its only real request across the whole month was an LPG top-up, which they were able to find along the route.

Tromsø · the unplanned highlight

The best decision in Tromsø was one they had barely planned.

When the family reached Tromsø, they decided to see whether they could find somewhere offering dog sledding. It was not one of the things they had driven north to do. They simply wondered if they could find somewhere offering it once they arrived.

It became one of the best experiences of the month. The kids loved it. The dogs seemed to love pulling the sleds. Afterwards the family sat inside traditional Norwegian huts and ate reindeer stew, warming up after a day that had not really been part of the plan at all.

An unplanned day: dogs, sleds, snow and the hut afterwards.

That may be the best argument for not planning every hour of a road trip. Have a route, but leave enough room to say yes when something better appears.

What the kids remember

Some of the biggest memories were almost ridiculous.

There were landmarks, fjords and huge Arctic landscapes. Then there was Harley somehow floating down a river while standing on a huge block of ice. And Mia completely face-planting into the snow while trying to make snow angels.

Harley, a river and one enormous block of ice.
A family moment in the snow.

They are tiny moments next to a 6,500 km route, yet they explain why the trip still matters four years later. The family did not just visit Norway. They lived together in the van for a month, waking up somewhere different almost every day.

“For the rest of it, the van was our home and Norway was our back garden.”
Billy · reflecting on using only two campsites across the 30-day trip

Billy remembers Norway as one of the most welcoming countries they have experienced for travelling outdoors. Along the way they found places with practical touches such as cooking areas, free firewood and composting toilets. Only two nights of the entire month were spent on campsites.

The scenery was extraordinary. Preikestolen, Lofoten and Tromsø lived up to the anticipation. But when Billy talks about why the trip was special, he returns to something simpler: the freedom of the four of them being together, moving every day and letting the van turn a whole country into home.

Planning notes · checked 23 August 2026

What to know before an April road trip through Norway.

Billy's story is from 2022. These practical notes are a separate, current planning layer based on official Norwegian sources, so recheck conditions and rules before travelling.

1

Treat April as a conditions-first trip.

Norway's road authority says drivers are responsible for sufficient grip all year. In Nordland, Troms and Finnmark, winter tread rules run through 30 April, and winter tyres, chains or similar can be necessary whenever conditions demand them. Check your vehicle's weight-specific rules. Statens vegvesen tyre guidance ↗

2

Check roads, not just the route.

Use the Norwegian Public Roads Administration's live traffic information for closures, rough-weather roads, webcams and mountain pass status. A route that looks simple on a map can change quickly with snow and wind. Live road information ↗

3

Do not confuse right to roam with a right to park anywhere.

Visit Norway makes the distinction clear: the right to roam applies to people, not unrestricted vehicle parking. Respect signs, avoid uncultivated land, keep at least 150 metres from houses and cabins, and generally do not stay in the same place more than two nights without permission. Current camping guidance ↗

4

Recreate the sunrise carefully.

Preikestolen can be hiked year-round, but winter conditions can mean snow, ice, wind and rapidly changing weather. The official trail guidance recommends appropriate winter equipment and says guided hikes are sensible for people unfamiliar with winter hiking. There are no railings at the plateau. Preikestolen safety guidance ↗

5

Dog sledding can still work in April.

Visit Tromsø says snow-based dog sledding is usually available from November to April, depending on snow conditions and the operator. Book around your dates rather than assuming it will run throughout the month. Visit Tromsø ↗

6

Leave room for the unplanned bit.

This one comes from Billy, not a tourism board. The dog sledding became one of the family's favourite days precisely because it was not the reason they had driven to Tromsø. Build enough flexibility into the route to say yes to something you find once you arrive.

Your Norway campervan trip checklist

A practical starting list, not a substitute for checking current legal, ferry and road requirements for your own vehicle.

✓Confirm tyres, tread and cold-weather equipment for your vehicle and dates.
✓Check live road conditions and mountain passes before each major driving day.
✓Save ferry crossings, fuel and LPG options before remote sections.
✓Build a route that can flex when weather or road conditions change.
✓Check legal overnight parking rather than assuming every scenic pull-in is suitable.
✓Pack layers, waterproofs, sturdy footwear and traction suitable for planned hikes.
✓Book date-sensitive experiences such as dog sledding around actual availability.
✓Give the trip breathing room for the stop you did not know you wanted.
